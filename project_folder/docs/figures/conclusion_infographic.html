Read the full conclusion

Political violence is not merely a humanitarian concern but a structural barrier to economic development. Across 46 African countries over 27 years, three consistent findings emerge. Higher conflict intensity directly raises agricultural employment – high-conflict countries hold eight to ten percentage points more workers on farms. A clear threshold appears at roughly sixty conflict events per year; below it, economies can still shift labour into industry and services, but above it, structural transformation stalls. The effect lingers for two to three years after violence subsides, rendering short-term recovery inadequate. Conflict destroys infrastructure and depletes human capital through displacement and school closures, trapping workers in low-productivity agriculture. Reducing violence yields measurable returns: each percentage point decline in agricultural employment translates into hundreds of thousands of non-farm jobs. For governments and donors, the evidence calls for targeted conflict reduction in hotspot countries such as Nigeria and Sudan, using the threshold as an early-warning signal, and committing to multi-year post-conflict support. Peace is not only a moral imperative – it is a practical economic strategy for job creation and structural transformation.

Sources: ACLED, World Bank, V-Dem (1996-2023)
